## 0. Mission Briefing

You are building **AURORA** — a working proof-of-concept that demonstrates how an AI system could have detected and prevented the April 28, 2025 Iberian Peninsula blackout, the worst power failure in modern European history, minutes before it happened.

This is not a toy chart. The goal is a genuinely impressive, technically grounded simulation-and-intervention demo: a live digital twin of a small power grid, seeded with the real physical conditions that caused the actual blackout, showing a "prevented" timeline next to the "actual" timeline, with an AI layer that spots the danger building and takes corrective action faster than any human operator could react.

**What this is:** a simulated, self-contained proof-of-concept built entirely on open-source power system simulators and public data, intended as a pitch/demo artifact to open conversations with utilities (Iberdrola, Red Eléctrica) and their innovation programs.

**What this is explicitly NOT:** this MVP is never to be connected to real grid infrastructure, real SCADA/EMS systems, or any live control system. All data is either synthetic (generated by the simulator) or pulled from public historical/real-time data APIs for realism grounding. Do not write code that implies, claims, or is designed to interface with real utility control systems. Any "autonomous action" in this MVP acts only on the simulated model, never on anything real. Be explicit about this distinction in the UI itself (a visible "SIMULATION" watermark/badge) so the demo is never mistaken for something it isn't.

## 1. The Real Incident You Are Modeling

Everything about this MVP's realism comes from grounding it in exactly what happened on April 28, 2025. Model this faithfully — the specific numbers below are what make the demo credible to someone who knows the domain.

### 1.1 What happened, in order

| Time (CEST) | Event |
|---|---|
| 12:03–12:07 | First low-frequency oscillation (~0.6 Hz) appears between Iberia and the wider Continental European grid. Detected and damped by operators. |
| 12:07 | Operator reduces the France–Spain interconnector export from 1,500 MW to 800 MW in response. |
| 12:16–12:22 | A second, lower-frequency oscillation appears (~0.2 Hz, a classic inter-area mode). Voltage fluctuations of roughly 30 kV are recorded on the 400 kV network. The France–Spain HVDC link is switched to constant-power mode. Operators bring another synchronous generator online and believe the grid has stabilized. |
| 12:32:57 | First generation trip: ~317 MW of distributed generation disconnects, apparently on a voltage-protection trip, near southern Spain. |
| 12:33:16–12:33:18 | Two more large trips follow within two seconds of each other: ~730 MW and ~550 MW. Total generation lost in under 30 seconds: on the order of 1,600–2,200 MW (sources vary on exactly what's included in the total; both figures are cited below). |
| 12:33:18–12:33:21 | Frequency collapses below 48.0 Hz. Automatic under-frequency load shedding (UFLS) fires across the Iberian Peninsula — but voltage keeps rising even as frequency falls, an unusual and dangerous combination. |
| 12:33:20–12:33:21 | Protection relays on the France–Spain AC interconnectors trip to prevent loss of synchronism, electrically islanding Iberia from the rest of Europe. |
| 12:33:24 | Total system collapse. The France–Spain HVDC link also stops transmitting. |

**Impact:** ~31 GW of load disconnected (≈25.2 GW in Spain, ≈5.9 GW in Portugal). Most of the peninsula was dark for around 10 hours; some areas longer. Eight deaths were linked to the outage (seven in Spain, one in Portugal), with more than 25 injuries reported. Full restoration: Portugal by 00:22 CEST on April 29; Spain by roughly 04:00 CEST the same night, achieved through black-start procedures beginning with Spanish hydropower plants at 13:30 and Portuguese black-start facilities activated in sequence from 16:11–17:26, with international interconnections progressively restored from 18:36–21:35.

### 1.2 What actually caused it (this is the part that matters for your simulation)

The official ENTSO-E Expert Panel investigation and independent technical analyses converge on a specific, nuanced root cause — and it is **not** "too much solar and wind," which is the popular misconception. Get this right, because it's the whole basis of the product:

The system experienced an unusual and dangerous combination: **frequency falling while voltage was rising** — normally these move together. This happened because the generators still running were **under-excited** (a generator can be dialed to either produce or absorb reactive power; under-excited units were still producing active power but had been configured or drifted into a state where they weren't absorbing the excess reactive power the grid needed pulled out of it). That is a **reactive power / voltage control failure**, not a renewable-penetration failure. In fact, the investigators explicitly noted that on the day of the blackout, renewable penetration and inertia were both within normal operating parameters — system inertia was at 2.3 seconds, right at the regulatory minimum, but not below it, and the generation mix (roughly 59% solar, 12% wind at the time) was typical for an April midday.

The real gaps investigators identified:

- **Reactive power was known to be a problem for years.** The operator had proposed a reactive-power compensation payment scheme as early as July 2022 — this was a known, unaddressed structural weakness, not a surprise.
- **Protection relays were miscalibrated.** Overly sensitive, inconsistent voltage-trip thresholds on generation assets caused unnecessary disconnections as voltage oscillated, which is what turned a stressed-but-survivable grid into a cascading collapse.
- **Oscillation damping was inadequate.** The Continental European synchronous grid has increasingly under-damped, low-frequency inter-area oscillation modes (the 0.6 Hz and 0.2 Hz events above) as more spinning, inertia-providing synchronous generators are displaced by inverter-based renewables that don't naturally resist sudden changes the way spinning machines do.
- **Under-frequency load shedding made voltage worse, not better.** UFLS is designed to fix frequency problems by shedding load, but shedding load in a system that's already over-voltage can push voltage even higher — a coordination gap between frequency and voltage protection schemes (this maps directly onto NERC/FERC standard PRC-006-1, which explicitly requires utilities to plan for overvoltage conditions *after* UFLS actions, not assume load shedding alone stabilizes a grid).
- **The most important gap for your product's thesis: visibility.** Independent post-event analysis found that PMUs (the high-speed sensors that would have given operators a clear, sub-second view of what was happening) near the failure site were offline, and official high-resolution measurements from the grid operators have still not been made available to the public or research community. The people in the control room did not have a clear enough real-time picture of reactive power margins and oscillation modes to catch this in time. **This is the exact gap AURORA is built to close.**
- This wasn't a one-off freak event either — a Chilean blackout in February 2025 showed a strikingly similar voltage/frequency dynamic, suggesting this is an emerging pattern as grids worldwide add more inverter-based generation, not an isolated Spanish problem.

### 1.3 The specific numbers to bake into your simulation

Use these as your target parameters so the demo is defensible in a technical conversation, not just visually impressive:

- Two precursor oscillations: ~0.6 Hz (12:03–12:07) and ~0.2 Hz (12:16–12:22), both classic **inter-area oscillation modes** — this is precisely the phenomenon the textbook Kundur two-area four-machine benchmark system was built to demonstrate, which is why it's your ideal starting simulation (see Section 3).
- System inertia at or near the regulatory floor: **2.3 seconds**.
- Renewable generation mix around the event: **~59% solar, ~12% wind**.
- Spain's grid code requires renewable generators to operate within a **±0.98 power factor** band — this is a real, enforceable constraint you can encode directly into your generator reactive-power limits.
- Three generation trips within about 20 seconds, roughly **317 MW, 730 MW, and 550 MW** (some sources cite a rounder total of ~2,200 MW including additional distributed generation that tripped automatically — present both figures with their caveats rather than asserting false precision).
- Voltage surged to roughly **1.14 per-unit** in places (reported as up to ~239 kV on part of the 220 kV network) during the final collapse phase — a serious, sustained overvoltage condition, not a brief transient.
- Frequency collapse below **48.0 Hz** triggered automatic UFLS.
- Total cascade-to-collapse time from the first generation trip to total system collapse: **about 27 seconds** (12:32:57 to 12:33:24). This is your key dramatic number — no human operator reacts meaningfully in 27 seconds; software can.

## 2. Product Concept & the User Experience You're Building Toward

The demo needs one unmistakable "wow" moment: **watching the same 27 seconds play out twice, side by side** — once as it actually happened (grid collapses, map goes dark), and once with AURORA watching and intervening (grid absorbs the shock and stays lit). Everything else in the build supports that moment.

Concretely, build toward these experience beats:

1. **A living grid.** A network diagram of buses, generators, and lines (start with the Kundur two-area system's 4 generators / 2 areas, see Section 3), rendered as a calm, glowing network when healthy — soft blue-green pulses moving along the lines like a heartbeat, timed to actual power flow.
2. **The invisible becoming visible.** A "vitals" panel showing live voltage, frequency, reactive power margin, and oscillation amplitude as smooth animated time-series — specifically including a "reactive power margin" gauge, since the whole point of this product is making an invisible, unmonitored quantity visible before it becomes a crisis. As the simulated event begins, this margin visibly drains toward zero while a human-operator's "traditional SCADA view" (slow, 4-second-refresh, sparse) sits right next to it showing almost nothing — this contrast is the entire sales pitch in one screenshot.
3. **A countdown, not just an alert.** Once AURORA's model detects the fragile-regime signature, show a literal countdown: "T-minus 23 seconds to cascade" ticking down, so the viewer feels the stakes in real time, mirroring the real 27-second collapse window.
4. **The fork in the road.** At the moment the real event's first generation trip would have occurred, split the view: LEFT plays out exactly what happened historically (cascading trips, protection islanding, total collapse, the network diagram going dark, echoing real outage maps of Spain and Portugal). RIGHT shows AURORA intervening — a visible "correction pulse" rippling outward from the affected generators (adjusting excitation/reactive power output, retuning the too-sensitive protection thresholds in real time, dispatching any modeled storage) — and the grid absorbing the shock, oscillations damping out, lights staying on.
5. **Instant forensics.** Immediately after either outcome, auto-generate a one-page incident report (what was detected, when, what action was recommended or taken, what the outcome was) — a callback to the fact that it took ENTSO-E's real investigation many months to produce their final report, and AURORA produces the equivalent in seconds.
6. **A credibility layer.** Somewhere in the UI, pull real, live or historical Spanish grid data (via ENTSO-E/ESIOS, Section 5) and show it quietly alongside the simulation — e.g., "today's actual Spanish generation mix" ticking in the corner. It signals this isn't a pure toy model; it's connected to the real system's rhythms even though the fault scenario itself is simulated.

## 3. System Architecture

Five components, in order of build priority:

**A. Simulation engine** — a power system dynamic model that can represent a small grid, run time-domain simulation, and have faults/disturbances injected into it programmatically. This is the ground truth generator for everything downstream.

**B. Scenario / fault-injection layer** — code that configures the simulation engine to reproduce the specific conditions in Section 1.3 (reduced inertia, under-excited generators, timed generation trips) and can also run a "counterfactual" branch where corrective parameters are applied instead.

**C. Detection / early-warning layer** — consumes the simulator's output stream (acting as if it were live PMU data) and predicts, ahead of the actual collapse, that the system is entering a dangerous regime. Start rule-based/statistical for the MVP (see Section 6), with a documented path to the more sophisticated graph neural network approach as the "v2" story for the pitch.

**D. Corrective-action layer** — once triggered, computes and applies a corrective intervention back into the simulation engine (reactive power redispatch, adaptive protection threshold, simulated storage/curtailment dispatch), producing the "prevented" counterfactual timeline.

**E. Presentation layer** — the real-time dashboard: the living network diagram, the vitals panel, the countdown, the split-screen replay, and the auto-generated report. This is streamed from a backend process that's advancing the simulation clock and pushing state updates to the frontend.

Data flows one direction, in a loop, at simulation-clock speed (not necessarily real wall-clock speed — you'll want to be able to run this faster or slower than real time for demo pacing): Simulation Engine → Detection Layer → (if triggered) Corrective-Action Layer → back into Simulation Engine → Presentation Layer, every tick.

## 4. Technology Stack — Exactly What to Use, and Why

### 4.1 Core dynamic simulation engine: **ANDES** (Python)

Use [ANDES](https://github.com/CURENT/andes) (`pip install andes`) as the core engine. This isn't an arbitrary choice — a 2024 academic survey comparing open-source power system dynamic simulators specifically for machine-learning applications ("A Survey of Open-Source Power System Dynamic Simulators with Grid-Forming Inverter for Machine Learning Applications") found ANDES and Julia's PSID.jl to be the two strongest options, and recommended ANDES specifically for being native Python, having fully documented parameters, and being easy to drive programmatically for rapid parameter sweeps — exactly what you need to both generate training scenarios and drive a live interactive demo.

Critically, ANDES has built-in **grid-forming inverter models** (GFM-Droop, GFM-VSM, and GFM-dVOC control types) — meaning you can explicitly model modern inverter-based renewable generation instead of only classic synchronous machines, which is essential for authentically reproducing a low-inertia, renewables-heavy grid rather than faking it with a legacy textbook model.

ANDES also ships a **built-in Kundur two-area, four-machine test system** (`andes.get_case('kundur/kundur_full.xlsx')`) — this is the single most important simulation asset for this project. It is the textbook benchmark system used across power engineering specifically to study exactly the kind of lightly-damped, low-frequency inter-area oscillations (in the 0.1–1 Hz range) observed in the real event's ~0.6 Hz and ~0.2 Hz oscillations. Start here before considering a larger network.

Basic ANDES workflow to build from:

```python
import andes
andes.config_logger(stream_level=20)

# Load the built-in Kundur two-area benchmark system
ss = andes.load(andes.get_case('kundur/kundur_full.xlsx'))

# Solve initial power flow (steady state)
ss.PFlow.run()

# Configure and run a time-domain (dynamic) simulation
ss.TDS.config.tf = 30      # simulate 30 seconds, matching the real cascade window
ss.TDS.run()

# Output: rotor speeds (omega) show inter-area oscillations,
# bus voltage magnitudes/angles, line power flows — all in .npz/.txt for analysis
```

The Kundur case includes a pre-built line-trip disturbance mechanism you'll extend into your own scripted fault sequence (Section 6, Phase 2) that mimics the three real generation trips, timed at the real intervals, plus an under-excitation parameter change to reproduce the reactive-power failure mode.

For a bigger, closer-to-real-Spain network once the core mechanic is proven, the **IEEE 39-bus New England system** (10 generators, 39 buses — also explicitly used in the literature for inter-area oscillation studies) is available from the Texas A&M Electric Grid Test Case Repository (registration required, free) and can be imported into ANDES via MATPOWER/PSS/E format converters ANDES already supports. Treat this as a Phase 7 stretch goal, not a Phase 1 requirement — don't let network size become a blocker before the core mechanic works.

### 4.2 Secondary/supporting tools

**pandapower** (`pip install pandapower`) — a mature, well-documented Python power-flow and short-circuit analysis library. Use it if/when you want steady-state network topology analysis, reactive power compensation device modeling, or a simpler static "what would happen if we lost this line" check outside of full time-domain dynamics. It's a good complement to ANDES, not a replacement — ANDES gives you the dynamics (how things unfold over seconds), pandapower gives you quick steady-state cross-checks.

**PyPSA** — an open energy-system modeling library. Optional, but useful if you want to ground your simulated generation mix in the real historical Spanish dispatch mix (the ~59% solar / 12% wind figure) rather than an arbitrary one, by importing real ENTSO-E generation data as the starting dispatch condition for your ANDES scenario.

### 4.3 Real-data grounding: ENTSO-E Transparency Platform + Spain's ESIOS

These make the "credibility layer" (Section 2, beat 6) possible, and can also be used to parameterize realistic generation-mix scenarios.

**ENTSO-E Transparency Platform** (pan-European: generation by source, load, cross-border flows, day-ahead prices):
- Register an account at the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform, then email **transparency@entsoe.eu** with the subject line "Restful API access" to request API access (approval typically takes a few business days). Once approved, generate your token under My Account Settings → Web Api Security Token.
- Python client: `pip install entsoe-py`

```python
from entsoe import EntsoePandasClient
import pandas as pd

client = EntsoePandasClient(api_key='YOUR_API_KEY')

start = pd.Timestamp('20250428', tz='Europe/Madrid')
end = pd.Timestamp('20250429', tz='Europe/Madrid')

generation = client.query_generation('ES', start=start, end=end)  # generation by source, Spain
load = client.query_load('ES', start=start, end=end)
```

**ESIOS** (Red Eléctrica's own Spanish-market data platform — more granular, Spain-specific indicators like real-time demand and system prices):
- Request a personal API token by emailing **consultasios@ree.es**.
- Python client: `pip install pyesios` (see the `SanPen/ESIOS` GitHub library for a clean reference implementation), or REE's own indicator-based REST API directly.

```python
from ESIOS import *

token = 'YOUR_ESIOS_TOKEN'
esios = ESIOS(token)

indicators = [1293]  # 1293 = national demand (MW); consult ESIOS's indicator catalogue for others
df_multi, df_list, names = esios.get_multiple_series(indicators, start_date, end_date)
```

Use these for two things: (1) pulling the real historical Spanish generation mix to parameterize your scenario's starting dispatch realistically, and (2) a live "real grid, right now" ticker in the corner of the dashboard for credibility. Don't block Phase 1–3 of the build on getting these API keys approved — they're a polish/credibility layer, not a dependency for the core simulation-and-detection mechanic.

### 4.4 Detection / prediction layer

For the MVP, build a **rule-based / physics-threshold detector** first — this is not a cop-out, it's honest and defensible: real under-frequency and under-voltage load-shedding schemes in actual grids are rule-based too. Track, per simulation tick: rate-of-change-of-frequency (ROCOF), the reactive power margin (how close each generator is to its ±0.98 power factor limit), and oscillation amplitude/growth rate on bus voltage. Trigger the "danger" state and start the countdown when these cross thresholds derived from the real event's own numbers (e.g., reactive margin below some percentage of headroom, combined with a sustained low-frequency oscillation growing rather than damping).

For the "v2 / roadmap" story to tell in the pitch — and to build toward if time allows — reference this specific published approach: **"Power Failure Cascade Prediction using Graph Neural Networks"** (arXiv:2404.16134, MIT). It represents the grid as a graph, takes the topology, an initial contingency (which branch/line fails first), and node-level power injections as input, and uses a "flow-free" attention-based GNN (no explicit power-flow recalculation at each step) to predict, per branch, a probability distribution over *when* it will fail — from which the full cascade sequence is reconstructed. Its key advantage: a single trained model generalizes across different loading levels, unlike baselines that need retraining per scenario. This is the credible, cite-able "here's the sophisticated version we're building toward" answer if a technical audience asks how the detection layer really works at scale.

### 4.5 Presentation layer

For speed and to stay inside what's realistically buildable as an MVP: a **Python backend (FastAPI) streaming simulation state over a WebSocket**, feeding a lightweight frontend built with **Plotly.js or D3** for the time-series "vitals" panel and a simple animated SVG/Canvas network diagram (nodes = buses/generators colored by voltage stress, edges = lines colored/animated by power flow and oscillation). This is well within a single build session's reach and avoids a heavier framework/toolchain.

If there's appetite to go further on visual polish later, **deck.gl** is a proven choice specifically for power grid visualization — the open-source PyPSA-Earth project built exactly this kind of dashboard (Next.js + TypeScript + Tailwind + deck.gl) for visualizing energy network state. Treat that as the Phase 7+ "make it beautiful for the actual pitch meeting" upgrade, not the Phase 1 target.

## 5. Synthetic PMU Data — the Schema to Standardize On

Since the entire product thesis is "we see what existing monitoring missed," make your simulated sensor data look like real synchrophasor data, following the actual **IEEE C37.118.2** standard used by real Phasor Measurement Units. Each simulated PMU "reading," emitted at a fixed rate, should carry:

- A GPS-synchronized timestamp
- Voltage phasor (magnitude + phase angle, or rectangular real/imaginary) per monitored bus
- Current phasor (magnitude + phase angle) per monitored branch
- Frequency (Hz)
- ROCOF — rate of change of frequency (Hz/s)
- Analog channels (optional: e.g., real/reactive power)
- Digital status flags (breaker/relay status)
- A data-quality/status word (real PMUs flag data validity — worth including since the real event's investigation explicitly noted PMUs near the fault were offline; you can dramatize this by having your "traditional SCADA" comparison view show a stale/missing-data state at the critical moment)

Real PMUs report at standardized rates of 10/25/50 readings per second (50 Hz systems, which Spain uses) or 10/12/15/30/60 (60 Hz systems). Emit your simulated stream at one of these standard rates (e.g., 50 fps) rather than an arbitrary rate — it's a small detail that reads as informed rather than invented when someone in the field looks at your code.

## 6. Build Plan — Phases with Acceptance Criteria

**Phase 0 — Environment setup.** Python 3.10+, `pip install andes pandapower fastapi uvicorn websockets numpy pandas`. Confirm ANDES runs its own bundled example (`andes run kundur_full.xlsx` or the Python API above) and produces output before writing any custom code. *Acceptance: a plot of rotor speed oscillations from the unmodified Kundur case, proving the toolchain works end to end.*

**Phase 1 — Baseline healthy grid.** Wire the ANDES Kundur two-area simulation into a script that runs a steady 30-second window with no disturbance, streaming bus voltage, frequency, and generator rotor angles to a simple logged output. *Acceptance: a stable, undisturbed time series — this is your "everything normal" baseline the rest of the demo will deviate from.*

**Phase 2 — Recreate the real fault.** Modify the scenario to (a) reduce system inertia toward the real 2.3-second figure by adjusting generator inertia constants or swapping a subset of synchronous machines for ANDES's grid-forming inverter models, (b) push one or more generators toward under-excitation (reduce reactive power output/AVR setpoint) to reproduce the real voltage-rise-with-falling-frequency signature, and (c) script three sequential trip events at roughly t, t+19s, and t+21s (mirroring the real ~317/730/550 MW trips 20 seconds apart) that remove generation capacity. Let this scenario run uncorrected to completion. *Acceptance: your simulation reproduces the qualitative signature of the real event — voltage rising while frequency falls, oscillation growing rather than damping, cascading trips, eventual collapse — even if the exact MW/kV numbers don't match perfectly. Qualitative fidelity to the mechanism matters more than decimal-point accuracy here.*

**Phase 3 — Detection layer.** Build the rule-based detector (Section 4.4) consuming the Phase 2 output stream tick-by-tick (not with hindsight/future knowledge) and firing a "danger" flag with meaningfully more lead time than a real operator watching a slow SCADA feed would have. *Acceptance: the detector fires before the first real generation trip in your simulated timeline, with a visible countdown to the point of collapse — this lead time is your core value proposition, so log and display it explicitly (e.g., "detected 23 seconds before cascade").*

**Phase 4 — Corrective-action layer.** On detection, branch the simulation: apply a corrective intervention (restore reactive power support on the under-excited generator(s), relax/retune the overly sensitive protection trip threshold, and/or dispatch a modeled battery storage unit if you've added one) and continue the simulation forward. *Acceptance: side-by-side, the uncorrected branch collapses as in Phase 2, and the corrected branch damps the oscillation and survives — a genuine counterfactual, not a scripted animation.*

**Phase 5 — Real-time dashboard.** Build the FastAPI/WebSocket backend and the frontend (network diagram, vitals panel, countdown, split-screen replay) described in Section 2. *Acceptance: a non-technical viewer can watch the demo once and immediately understand what happened, why, and what AURORA did differently — this is the actual pitch-meeting artifact.*

**Phase 6 — Forensic auto-report.** After any run, generate a short structured report (timestamps of detection, thresholds crossed, action taken, outcome) as Markdown or PDF. *Acceptance: a one-page document that reads like the plain-English executive summary of an ENTSO-E-style investigation, generated instantly rather than in months.*

**Phase 7 — Credibility & polish.** Wire in the ENTSO-E/ESIOS live data ticker (Section 4.3), consider migrating the visualization to the richer deck.gl-based stack if time allows, and optionally extend the network from the 4-machine Kundur system to the 39-bus New England system for a "this scales" argument. *Acceptance: the deck is demo-ready for a real conversation with an innovation-hub contact, not just a personal proof of concept.*

## 8. Guardrails (Keep These Explicit in the Code and the UI)

Label all data sources clearly in the UI: "SIMULATED" for anything from the ANDES model, "PUBLIC HISTORICAL/LIVE DATA" for anything from ENTSO-E/ESIOS. Never let the demo imply a live connection to Iberdrola, Red Eléctrica, or any real control system — it has none, and shouldn't. When you eventually pitch this, the honest and more persuasive framing is: "here is a working demonstration of the detection and correction mechanism on realistic simulated physics; a real pilot would start with read-only access to a slice of your actual PMU data in advisory-only mode, with closed-loop automatic control being a much later, earned step."
